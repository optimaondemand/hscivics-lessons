Constitution of Athens

Attributed to Aristotle, ca. 330 BC

Reading: An account of how Athens moved from a narrow rule of birth toward government answerable to its citizens: the beginning of an extended study of what a citizen is, and why the American founders kept returning to Athens and Rome for their answer.

🎭 Before We Begin

It is 594 BC, and Athens stands at the edge of civil war. You are a free citizen, but not a wealthy one: your farm barely feeds your family, and last year's harvest fell short. Your neighbor holds the note on your debt, and if you cannot pay, the law allows him to seize you, your wife, and your children and force you to work his land until the debt is settled, or sell you outright. Thousands of farmers across the city face the same choice you do. The Aristocrats who hold every office of government want nothing to change. The poorest commoners want the land divided among them by force. Both sides have just agreed to hand the entire problem to one man, an archon named Solon, and given him the power to rewrite the city's constitution. No one yet knows what he will do with it.

Begin with a debt. Not a metaphor: an actual debt, owed by an actual farmer, to an actual man richer than he was. In Athens, in the years before 594 BC, a poor farmer who could not pay what he owed could be seized, along with his wife and children, and made to work the creditor's land as bond-servants. If the debt still wasn't paid, he could be sold outright. The Greeks had a word for this arrangement, hektemoroiA poor Athenian tenant farmer who owed a landlord a sixth of his harvest as rent, and could be seized as a bond servant if he fell short. , "sixth-parters," named for the sixth of the harvest a tenant owed his landlord as rent. Fail to pay that rent, and the law offered the landlord your body as security.

The dividing line in early Athens was not rich versus poor. It was Aristocrat versus commoner, and "commoner" covered a wide range of fortunes. Some commoners, especially those who had done well through trade, were wealthier still, in a few cases as wealthy as any Eupatrid family. What excluded all of them from office was not the size of their harvest. It was the accident of not having been born into the right handful of families.

Every political community answers the question who rules, and on what basis? differently, and Athens's answer, for its first two centuries, was simply: birth decides, because it always has. A commoner's wealth, however great, bought him no share in governing. That is not a constitutional theory. It is inertia given the appearance of order. The interesting history begins when Athens stopped accepting that answer.

The Archon Who Refused a Crown

By the early 590s BC, Athens stood at the edge of civil war. The poorest commoners, crushed by debt-bondage, wanted the land redivided. The Aristocracy, holding both the land and the offices of state, wanted nothing to change. Both sides, remarkably, agreed to hand the problem to one man: Solon, himself of aristocratic blood, though not from among the wealthiest of the great Eupatrid houses. "Moderate means," though, undersells him: Solon was a merchant who made his own fortune trading across the Aegean both before and after his archonship, journeys that were not the ventures of a poor man, and he was already known across the city for his poetry and his sharp tongue. He was elected archonOne of the chief magistrates who governed Athens; before Solon, the office was open only to the hereditary nobility. in 594 BC with something close to emergency powers: the authority to rewrite the constitution.

Solon did not give the poorest commoners what they wanted most (a redistribution of the land) nor the Aristocracy what it wanted most (no change at all). Instead he cancelled the existing debts outright (a measure the Athenians called the SeisachtheiaSolon's "shaking off of burdens": the cancellation of Athenian debts and the ban on using a person's own body as loan collateral. , the "shaking-off of burdens") and permanently outlawed the practice of using a person's own body as collateral for a loan. No Athenian could again be enslaved for debt. He went further still: where Athenians had already been sold into slavery abroad before his reforms took effect, the state tracked down a number of them, purchased their freedom at public expense, and brought them home. He then reorganized eligibility for office around a new principle: not birth, but a graduated scale of wealth. ⚡ Try it · Whose burden?
Solon's reforms lifted different burdens from different people. Match each person to the reform that answers his problem.
1. A tenant farmer has fallen behind on the sixth of his harvest that he owes, and fears the landlord will seize him and his family.
2. A merchant of common birth has grown wealthy and wants a say in governing the city.
3. A landless laborer is treated unjustly by a magistrate and wants someone to hear his appeal.
4. An Athenian was sold abroad as a slave for debt before the reforms took effect.

Notice what Solon did not do. He did not make Athens a democracy in the sense we would use the word. Office-holding still depended on property, and the Eupatridae still dominated the archonship and the AreopagusThe council of former archons that heard serious court cases and guarded the laws of Athens. in practice, since only the wealthy could afford the unpaid burden of office. Every male citizen, whatever his class, had always been entitled to attend the Assembly; that ancient institution was never in dispute, before Solon or after him. What Solon actually gave the Thetes, for the first time, was a vote on the jury that judged their neighbors and their magistrates. Aristotle, writing a century and a half later, singled out that right of appeal to a jury of one's peers as the single most consequential of Solon's reforms, more consequential, he thought, even than cancelling the debts, because it planted the seed from which everything recognizably democratic in Athens later grew.

When his work was done, Solon did something almost no one in his position ever does: he left. Athenian law let him rule as he liked for as long as he liked, and both the Aristocracy and the poorest commoners by turns tried to talk him into becoming their champion against the other. He refused both offers, left the city for ten years so that no one could pressure him to revise his own laws, and let Athens live under what he had written, unrevised by its author. He later wrote that he had "stood with my shield thrown round both sides alike, and let neither triumph unjustly over the other," a self-description modern historians have generally found more accurate than self-serving.

A Question Older Than Athens, and Newer Than Yesterday

Every constitutional order has to answer a small set of recurring questions: Who rules, and what makes that rule legitimate? How is authority distributed among citizens and the communities they belong to? What makes people willingly obey the law and bring their disputes to the courts rather than settling them by force? This course will return to these questions in one form or another throughout the semester, through Rome, through Locke and Montesquieu, through Philadelphia in 1787, through the Federalist Papers, and eventually through the Bill of Rights and a string of Supreme Court cases that are still being argued about today.

Solon matters to that story for a specific reason. James Madison and the other framers of the U.S. Constitution were trained in classical literature the way modern students are trained in mathematics: it was assumed, foundational, everywhere. When Madison sat down in 1787 to think about how to distribute power in a government answerable to its citizens, he was not inventing the problem. He had Athens, and Rome after it, in view as case studies of what happens when a political community tries, and sometimes fails, to answer that same question well. Solon's Athens is the first surviving case in the Western tradition of a community that looked at its own crumbling legitimacy and chose reform over either revolution or repression. That is why we start here.
