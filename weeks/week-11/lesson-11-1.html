Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words below are worth reviewing before you read.

appellate jurisdictionA court's authority to review and revise the decision of a lower court, rather than hearing the case for the first time.
Good behaviourThe Constitution's standard for how long federal judges may serve: for as long as they behave well, in practice meaning for life unless impeached.
ordain and establishThe Constitution's own language, in Article III, for Congress's power to create the federal courts below the Supreme Court.
original jurisdictionThe authority of a court to hear a case first, before any other court has ruled on it.
pocket vetoAn indirect veto that occurs when the President neither signs nor returns a bill within ten days and Congress has adjourned, so the bill dies without a signature.
regular vetoThe President's ordinary veto: returning a bill to Congress unsigned, with objections, within ten days of receiving it.
treasonThe only crime the Constitution itself defines: levying war against the United States, or giving aid and comfort to its enemies.
unityIn Hamilton's Federalist No. 70 argument, having a single person, rather than a council or committee, hold the executive power, so that responsibility for action cannot be diffused or hidden.
vetoThe President's power to reject a bill passed by Congress, which Congress can override only with a two-thirds vote in both chambers.

🎭 Before We Begin

Read Article III on its own and you will notice what it does not do. It names one court, the Supreme Court, and stops. It does not say how many justices sit on it, does not create a single lower court, does not say where a federal trial will actually be held if you are ever party to one. The framers have handed the entire architecture of the federal court system to whatever the first Congress decides to build, and in 1789 that Congress has to start from nothing: no district courts, no circuit courts, no settled practice for how a case moves from a trial to an appeal. Everything you now think of as "the federal courts" is about to be invented by statute, not by the Constitution itself.

Article I took seven sections to build Congress. Article II took three to build the presidency. Article III builds the entire federal judiciary in three short sections, and the first sentence of the first section tells you why it could afford to be so brief: "The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish." The Constitution itself creates exactly one federal court. Everything else in the federal court system (district courts, circuit courts of appeals, the entire architecture most people picture when they picture "the federal courts") exists only because Congress chose to create it, and could in theory be reorganized by Congress again.

This was a deliberate design choice, not an oversight. The framers could not know in 1787 how much federal litigation the new government would generate, what regions would need courts, or how many judges any of it would require. Rather than guess and lock a specific court structure into the Constitution's text, they gave Congress the ongoing power to ordain and establishThe Constitution's own language, in Article III, for Congress's power to create the federal courts below the Supreme Court. whatever "inferior" courts (meaning simply lower courts, ranked beneath the Supreme Court in the judicial hierarchy, not courts of lesser dignity) the nation's business required. Congress used that power almost immediately: the Judiciary Act of 1789The first Congress's law establishing the federal court system, including the Supreme Court's initial size and the lower federal courts., one of the first laws the new Congress passed, created the federal district and circuit courts, set the size of the Supreme Court at six justices, and built the basic three-tiered structure that, much amended and expanded since, still organizes the federal judiciary today. Article III supplied the constitutional permission; the Judiciary Act supplied the actual courthouse.

The Federal Court Hierarchy
District Courts
Trial courts created by Congress; where a federal case is usually first heard and decided.
Circuit Courts of Appeals
Created by Congress to review a district court's decision for legal error, not to retry the facts.
The Supreme Court
The one court Article III itself creates; the final word on federal law, mostly through appellate review.

Two Protections You Have Already Met

Section 1 also states the two protections for federal judges that Module 4 examined in detail through Hamilton's Federalist No. 78: judges "shall hold their offices during good Behaviour," and shall receive a salary "which shall not be diminished during their Continuance in Office." Good behaviourThe Constitution's standard for how long federal judges may serve: for as long as they behave well, in practice meaning for life unless impeached. tenure means a federal judge, once confirmed, effectively serves for life; removable only through impeachment and conviction for genuine misconduct, not at the pleasure of whoever appointed them or whoever currently controls Congress. The salary protection means Congress cannot punish a judge for an unpopular ruling by cutting his pay. Recall Hamilton's argument: the judiciary, having "neither Force nor Will, but merely judgment," is naturally the weakest of the three branches, and needs the strongest institutional shelter to stay independent of the other two. Article III's text is where that argument became binding law. What Federalist No. 78 defended in theory in 1788, Article III had already written into the Constitution's actual text the year before.

Original Jurisdiction and Appellate Jurisdiction

Section 2 draws a distinction worth holding onto for the rest of the course: the difference between original jurisdictionThe authority of a court to hear a case first, before any other court has ruled on it., the authority to hear a case first, as a trial court, and appellate jurisdictionA court's authority to review and revise the decision of a lower court, rather than hearing the case for the first time., the authority to review a decision a lower court has already made. The Constitution gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction only in a narrow set of cases; those "affecting Ambassadors, other public Ministers and Consuls," and those in which a state itself is a party. A concrete example: if one state sues another state directly over a water-rights dispute along a shared river, that case can go straight to the Supreme Court as a trial, with no lower court involved at all, because Article III places state-versus-state disputes within the Court's original jurisdiction.

Every other category of case Article III lists reaches the Supreme Court, if it reaches the Court at all, through appellate jurisdiction; meaning a lower court decides the case first, and the losing party may ask the Supreme Court to review that decision, subject to whatever exceptions and regulations Congress imposes. A concrete example: a federal district court convicts a defendant of a federal crime; the defendant appeals to a circuit court of appeals; if the circuit court affirms the conviction, the defendant may ask the Supreme Court to review the case on appeal. The Court is not retrying the facts from scratch in this posture; it is reviewing whether the law was applied correctly below. The overwhelming majority of the Supreme Court's business, then and now, is appellate rather than original.

What Article III Places Within Federal Judicial Power

Section 2 also lists the categories of dispute the federal judicial power reaches: cases "arising under this Constitution, the Laws of the United States, and Treaties," cases affecting ambassadors and foreign ministers, admiralty and maritime cases, controversies between two or more states, controversies between a state and citizens of another state, and controversies between citizens of different states, among others. Two further guarantees deserve particular attention. Section 2 requires that "the Trial of all Crimes, except in Cases of Impeachment, shall be by Jury": the Constitution's original jury-trial guarantee for criminal cases, later joined and strengthened by the Sixth Amendment. Section 3 defines treasonThe only crime the Constitution itself defines: levying war against the United States, or giving aid and comfort to its enemies. against the United States narrowly and precisely, as "levying War" against the United States, or "adhering to their Enemies, giving them Aid and Comfort," and requires either the testimony of two witnesses to the same overt act or a confession in open court before anyone may be convicted of it. The framers had lived through the English Crown's habit of stretching treason accusations to silence political opponents; Article III's narrow, evidence-heavy definition was a deliberate guard against that same danger recurring under the new government.
